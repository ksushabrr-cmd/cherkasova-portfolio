What re-reading support tickets turned into revenue

Reading real support tickets — not the dashboard — found that roughly 30% of "fraud" wasn't fraud at all.

Context

The multi-account system treated every duplicate profile as a fraud risk. But roughly 30% of blocked users turned out to be legitimate — families sharing a device, sellers returning years after their first verification, people who'd simply lost access to an already-verified profile. Conversion to linking was stagnating, complaints were growing.

What I did

I read the raw support tickets myself before opening a dashboard — and found the real cause: not fraud growth, but bad segmentation. Ran regression analysis and in-depth interviews to separate legitimate patterns from abusive ones, then designed separate linking scenarios for each case — no phone number required if identity was already document-verified; linking allowed against a deleted profile on a document match. Security constraints stayed exactly where they needed to be.

"Everyone had written off deleted, already-verified profiles as a dead end. I found the pattern in the support queue, proved on data that unblocking them was cheap — and only then asked for engineering time."

03
Avito · Behavioral Research

The button that cried wolf

A panic button was crying wolf on genuine logins. I turned it into a real authentication factor.

+
Context

"This wasn't me" was meant to catch real account hijacks. Instead, false clicks — people confused by their own login, not attackers — drowned out genuine incidents and flooded support with tickets that weren't security problems at all. The old email asked two different questions at once and gave people no clean way to say "yes, that was me."

What I did

Ran a behavioral analysis of the false-positive clicks, then redesigned the confirmation flow across account settings, email and push notifications — one clear action instead of two competing ones, so it worked as a real authentication factor, not a panic button. Survey data fed back into retraining the fraud-labeling models, so the system got better at telling the two apart on its own.

05
Beeline · Service Design Manager · 2019–2021

The tariff nobody else was selling

A hundred studies kept surfacing the same complaint, so we built the tariff that fixed it.

+
Beeline · "Connection Z" configurator
Context

Over a year and a half I ran more than a hundred studies and usability tests — from the tariff storefront to family bundles. One insight kept repeating across segments: people were tired of paying a fixed subscription fee for a bundle they'd never use in full.

What I did

Segmentation and funnel diagnostics became the basis for the market's first tariff with no subscription fee — "Connection Z": pay only for what you actually use, nothing expires. Separately, redesigned the family tariff ecosystem based on real calling patterns, and redesigned the tariff storefront.

This is genuinely the case I'm proudest of — it's the one I show first.

How I Research
A lesson that stuck

Not every instinct survives contact with data. On one linking flow, worried about abuse, we made the scenario more complex to close a loophole — and lost real, legitimate users who had nothing to do with it. The fix wasn't more product ambition; it was going back to the data before trusting my own conviction.

How I run a study

Start with feedback and behavioral analytics, interviews, benchmarks. Then validate the hypothesis against real numbers and surveys before anyone draws a screen. I map the feature logic in BPMN myself when there's no systems analyst on the team, so engineering knows exactly what should happen on every click. Instrument the A/B test before it ships, not after, and roll out in slices when the change touches millions of users.
